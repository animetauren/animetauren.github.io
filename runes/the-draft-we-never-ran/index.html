the draft we never ran

tldr

  • The draft is better. NVIDIA's 4.21B drafter accepts 5.11–5.58 tokens per step against the 27B's own MTP head at 3.81–3.86, at every draft length measured.
  • It never ran. No load path in oMLX 0.8.0; the one Apple Silicon attempt lost to z-lab DFlash2; 252k physics project ~13–20 tok/s against a measured 46.5.
  • Acceptance isn't throughput. NVIDIA's speedup assumes LPX hardware that hides drafting behind parallel verification. One Mac has nowhere to hide it.

The card showed up on the seventeenth, and my instinct was the usual one: pull the weights, find a conversion, clear the afternoon for a benchmark. What stopped me was arithmetic.

This is part two of a short series. Part one was about a number everyone quotes that had never been measured properly: two Qwen checkpoints, one 256 GB Mac Studio, the full ~252k window. The 27B here is the dense Qwen3.8 from that post, with the same native 262,144-token context on the same machine. This one is about the experiment everyone would run next and why it didn't deserve the GPU time — the forty minutes before the afternoon, which is the part of the practice that doesn't produce a chart.

What they built. nvidia/Qwen3.5-4B-Qwen3.8-27B-Draft is a full standalone 4.21B language model in the qwen3_5 family, not a draft head: a distribution distillation of the 27B, trained on roughly 200k Nemotron prompts with the 27B's own sampled responses as the learning signal. Tokenizer and vocabulary are natively identical to the target, so there's no remapping step at decode. BF16 only, about 8.4 GB, trained at a sequence length of 12,288.

The numbers are the reason anyone noticed. Published acceptance is 5.11–5.58 tokens per step, against 3.81–3.86 for the 27B's embedded MTP head. It beats the MTP head at every draft length measured and is still improving past 16. For scale: the MTP head on this machine, at depth 4, measures 3.28–3.39 tokens per cycle at 76–78% acceptance. So the external draft is not marginally better at drafting. On acceptance alone it's the strongest drafter in reach, and I want to say that plainly before explaining why it doesn't come here.

Why it never ran. Four checks, each with a receipt, none of them a judgment about the model.

One: no load path. oMLX's DFlash draft interface expects hidden-state tappers — a drafter that reads the target's own hidden states. This is a token-only model with its own forward pass and its own KV cache. There is no MLX or oQ conversion; upstream is BF16-only, and the community quants that exist are GGUF and NVFP4, which belong to other runtimes. Loading it isn't a flag. It's a new code path, written and debugged against a 27B target.

Two: the only Apple Silicon integration measured it losing. oMLX prototype PR #5402 is the only place anyone has wired an autoregressive drafter into this stack. Unmerged, no server wiring, an open greedy-divergence bug at depth 2 — and in its own numbers it ran slower than the existing z-lab DFlash2 drafter on the same chip class, at short context. Short context is where a 4B standalone should shine: its attention state is cheap there, and the acceptance advantage isn't diluted. Lose there and long context isn't the arm where it recovers.

Three: the 252k physics — an estimate, not a measurement. Block-16 drafting means sixteen serial standalone forwards per cycle before a single verification pass. Each of those carries the draft's own full-width attention state, roughly 8 GB of it at 252k, and every byte is billed to the same 1.2 TB/s the 27B draws on for its 46.5 tok/s. The projection lands at ~13–20 tok/s. It is anchored by the measured context inversion in the point above, not by a stopwatch on this machine. The useful part of an estimate like that isn't precision; it's the sign and the margin. Even the optimistic end is 57% below the baseline.

Four: the warm-restore hazard. Issue #2384 is a GDN-sidecar walk-back bug, reproduced on this exact model family, serving cached_tokens=0 and truncating where it should have continued. The fix is unconfirmed in 0.8.0. Warm restore is the thing this whole setup exists to protect: a 252k prefix comes back in 1.14–2.83 s instead of 44.91 s cold on the 125B, and in 6.69 s on the 27B. A drafter that buys single-digit decode and puts that at risk isn't a trade. It's a different machine.

And the caveat is printed on the box. NVIDIA's own announcement frames the speedup around LPX — disaggregated hardware where "drafting time can be largely hidden" behind parallel verification — and notes that standalone GDN drafts are "generally unsupported in inference frameworks today." That isn't marketing small print. It's an honest statement of the deployment envelope, and the envelope doesn't include this desk.

The economics. This is the part I'd want a reader to keep, because it generalizes past one model card. Throughput is acceptance times cost: accepted tokens per step from a distribution match, steps per second from what a step costs on your hardware. The first number is a property of the model, and it's publishable. The second is a property of the machine and the context length, and it's the one that decides.

Acceptance says how close the draft's distribution sits to the target's, at some length, on some prompts. Cost per pass says where the work lands. On disaggregated hardware those are separable: one pool drafts, a bigger one verifies in parallel, and the drafting time hides. On a single bandwidth-bound Mac there is no second pool and no place to hide. Every draft token is a standalone forward, run serially ahead of verification, billed to the same 1.2 TB/s as the target's own weights and KV.

That's also why the MTP head is hard to beat here, and not because it's the better drafter. It's one extra layer riding the target's last hidden state — no second model, no second attention stack, almost no independent state. At depth 4 it returns 3.28–3.39 tokens per cycle at 76–78% acceptance, and it is cheap per draft. The 4B is a whole second model, a good one, paying its own attention at my context length, sixteen times per cycle.

So the draft wins the metric that's easy to publish and loses the one that's hard to measure. That isn't a paradox about NVIDIA's work; it's a topology mismatch, and it's visible before you touch a GPU. Acceptance times per-pass cost, with per-pass cost evaluated at your context length, on your machine. Three lines of arithmetic.

What I kept instead. A rejection is only worth something if it's written down, or it becomes a hunch that gets re-litigated every few weeks. So the candidate got a card: artifact, four points with receipts, verdict, watch-list, gates.

The watch-list has two entries. PR #5402 and any follow-up autoregressive-drafter work — re-reviewed once merged and server-wired, with the divergence bug closed. And an MLX conversion of the smaller sibling, Qwen3.5-0.8B-Qwen3.8-27B-Draft — about 1.5 GB, acceptance near MTP parity, and at that size the per-pass cost stops being fantasy. That one is physically plausible on this box. If someone converts it, it gets the afternoon.

And if the 27B ever gets a speculation trial at long context, the candidate isn't this one. It's z-lab's DFlash2 — or an oQ4 conversion of it — with adaptive verification and MTP off. The gates were agreed before any run, which is the point of gates:

  • pass — decode@252k ≥ 53.5 tok/s, at least +15% over the 46.5 baseline
  • pass — turn-2 warm TTFT ≤ 1.5× the MTP config
  • pass — served cached_tokens > 0 in the logs
  • pass — quality battery unchanged
  • kill — any walk-back or cached_tokens=0 signature
  • kill — warm TTFT regression worse than 2×
  • kill — decode@252k at or below 46.5

Note the weighting. Four of the seven conditions exist to protect the cache rather than to reward speed. That's correct on a machine whose value is a warm 252k restore. A drafter has to earn its place without disturbing the thing that makes deep context usable.

The other experiment. Part one promised two experiments that lost. The second was smaller: Jundot's oQ4h weights, the Hadamard-rotated mixed 4/5-bit twins of both Qwens. Roughly the same footprint, and the quality battery saturated at 10/10 on all four checkpoints — 27B and 125B, oQ4e and oQ4h — with nothing in task-level behavior to separate the formats. But the extra bit costs prefill: −17% on cold 252k on both models, −21% to −33% on short prompts, because the A8 prefill kernels cannot read oQh weights and the log says so at load. The 27B's decode@252k did improve, +1% to +6%, and it didn't buy back the prefill. Rejected as default, kept on disk as the quality-headroom option. Same shape of lesson: the axis being advertised wasn't the axis that decides.

Fairness. I don't want this to read as a reason NVIDIA's model is bad. It isn't. A 4.21B drafter distilled from a 27B's own sampled responses over 200k prompts, with the tokenizer matched natively so nothing has to be remapped, publishing 5.11–5.58 against 3.81–3.86 and stating which hardware the speedup assumes — that's careful work, reported honestly. On a rack where drafting runs on its own silicon and verification is parallel, the calculus inverts and I'd be writing a different post.

The reflex worth examining is temporal, not technical: benchmark first, topology later. It's the same reflex that let "Ollama is faster" travel in part one. A number was published, the number was true on somebody's machine, and nobody asked which machine before repeating it.

So the second experiment in this series is the one that didn't happen. Not because the model is weak. Because acceptance isn't throughput, and here the difference between them is sixteen serial forwards at 252k.

Forty minutes of reading, one card, four checks, seven gates, one projection labeled as a projection. If the gates ever pass, the afternoon is already budgeted. Until then, the cheapest experiment is the one you can rule out on paper.

Topology first. Then the afternoon.
