how ollama gets measured

tldr

  • It isn't faster here. At an audited 255k-token context, oMLX 0.8.0 beats Ollama 0.40.16 on cold TTFT (3.7–5.1×), prefill (~3.6×) and decode (41–63%), with quality 10/10 on both.
  • Ollama's one real win: 0.24 s repeat TTFT from a resident KV cache. It costs 149–155 GB and dies with the process.
  • The folklore is measurement foam: short-prompt benchmarks, silent 4k/32k defaults, MTP on by default, cache-warmed TTFT. The engine is good; the measuring isn't.

People say Ollama's MLX engine is faster than oMLX. It's said often enough to work as background knowledge — the kind of thing you inherit rather than check. I inherited it, then spent a day checking, because I had a 256 GB Mac Studio, two Qwen checkpoints I actually wanted to use, and a requirement that made the comparison unarguable: the full ~252k context, not a summary of it. This is the story of trying to get those two models to run as well as this hardware allows — through llama.cpp, Ollama, and finally oMLX.

The mission. Two specific checkpoints, one machine, and a hard requirement: the best performance I could get from each of them with the full ~252k window intact. Not a demo at 8k, not a benchmark at the context length the model doesn't actually need. The window was the constraint, and it disqualified more configurations than any speed number did.

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next is Qwen's experimental preview of the Qwen4 architecture (Qwen/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next): a vision-capable mixture of experts, 125B total parameters with only 6B active per token. It pairs Gated DeltaNet — linear attention that carries a recurrent state instead of a growing KV — with Qwen Sparse Attention for the layers that must look back at everything, and keeps a 51B-parameter n-gram embedding table on the side. Native context: 262,144 tokens.

Qwen3.8-27B is the dense sibling: ~27.8B parameters, the same GDN-hybrid layout, the same 262,144-token native window.

Both are served here as Jundot's oQ4e-mtp checkpoints — mixed-precision 4/5-bit MLX quantizations, ~100 GB and ~17.5 GB on disk, that preserve the model's own multi-token-prediction head. That last part is what makes speculative decoding free of a separate drafter: no second model, no second set of weights. Native context on both, no YaRN.

First: llama.cpp, tuned to the teeth, disqualified by the mission. The 125B ran there as a 104 GB UD-Q4_K_XL GGUF in four shards, under a launchd service, through twenty-eight logged one-variable experiments: batch and ubatch sizes, draft depths, n-gram speculation (mostly rejected, with the reasons written down), and the 1.0.0 update. The record is real — +48% prefill and +15–29% decode from tuning alone — finishing at 1,913 tok/s prefill, 107–123 tok/s decode on the agent loop, and ~430–500 ms append-turn TTFT where only a few dozen tokens of each turn were new.

It was also written at a 32,768-token context window — -c 32768 in the archived launcher — and that ended it. The engine that tuned best could not serve the window the mission needed. The numbers weren't the problem. The scope was.

Second: Ollama, first to serve the whole window. Its MLX engine (v0.40.x) served 262,144 tokens natively by default — the first engine on this box that could run the actual requirement rather than a fraction of it. The build is qwen3.8-flash-next:125b-mlx, NVFP4, 97.63 GiB: a complete checkpoint with nothing stripped, about 5 GiB larger on disk than the oQ4e it gets measured against, and its engine keeps the 26.82 GiB n-gram table host-backed rather than resident in the GPU set.

The receipts come from this machine. Its November 10 log shows chunked prefill holding 2,066–2,076 tok/s at 127k depth — above the 1,913 the tuned llama.cpp stack topped out at, and no later llama.cpp row beat it, including after 1.0.0. For deep prompts that is the number that matters, and it was the first evidence that the window and the speed could coexist.

The other half, because the next beat only earns its credibility if this one is written: llama.cpp kept a decode edge at short range, 107–123 tok/s against Ollama's 46–63. Ollama won the mission — a native window, and prefill at depth — not every metric.

Third: the folklore, and the foam. "Ollama's MLX engine is faster" became the default sentence on Apple Silicon, and it stayed true to everyone who repeated it because nobody re-measured it. The matched head-to-head below is the answer: three 252k prompts audited to the token, a 10/10 quality tie.

The mechanisms are in the body of this post with their receipts — a published benchmark that is a short-prompt run on a different model class (Qwen3.5-35B-A3B); v0.40.16 notes admitting /v1/responses "no longer silently truncates by default"; 4k and 32k context defaults on smaller machines that make a nominal 252k run secretly a 4k run; MTP on by default at adaptive depth 0→7, with their own 27B MTP variant carrying a documented 2×-slower report; cache-warmed TTFT quoted as general speed; and one Ultra-class head-to-head that began by claiming Ollama faster and was corrected by its own author to oMLX ~1.7×.

No single number in that list is a lie. The pipeline of quoted numbers lies about the aggregate. The engine is good; the measurement is the problem.

The machine. oMLX 0.8.0, from the DMG rather than Homebrew — Xcode isn't on this box, and the DMG is the only build that ships the custom kernels. All five were verified in the bundle: attention, lowbit, oqh, quant_matmul, sparse_index. A8 INT8 prefill on, Lightning MTP at depth 3 for the 125B and depth 4 for the 27B, and a tiered cache: 64 GB hot, 400 GB of paged SSD behind it.

Depth 4 wasn't inherited. On the 27B it measured better than depth 3: the fourth draft is accepted 79–86% of the time, and depth 3 saves about 2 GB. On a 256 GB machine that isn't a trade worth making.

What it buys, measured with server-side counters at temperature 0, thinking off:

  • 125B — 10.9k prompt: TTFT 1.74 s, prefill 6,269–6,272 tok/s, decode 144–147. At 252k cold: TTFT 44.91 s, prefill 5,688 tok/s, decode 82–86, peak wired 133.3 GB, swap flat. At 252k warm: restore 1.14–2.83 s, decode 90.7–91.1.
  • 27B — 10.9k prompt: TTFT 4.25 s, prefill 2,574 tok/s, decode 101–104. At 252k cold: TTFT 254–257 s, prefill ~1,000 tok/s, decode 46.5–46.7, wired 61–65 GB steady after an 81.8 GB first fill. Warm restore 6.69 s.
  • The ladder between — 32k in 4.81 s, 128k in 20.73 s, 252k in 44.91 s, with prefill easing 6,648 → 6,184 → 5,688 tok/s.

The dense 27B is ~5.7× slower than the 125B MoE on cold prefill and ~1.8× on decode. The cache rescues TTFT, not decode. The 125B is the deep-context tool; the 27B is the fast resident.

The warm number is the one I'd defend hardest. A 252k prefix restores from disk in 1.14–2.83 s. After a full server restart and a reboot, the first 252k hit came back in 2.28 s — about 19× faster than cold, with the log to prove it (cached=255,709, blocks=32). That's the difference between a deep-context machine you use and one you avoid.

The comparison. Ollama 0.40.16, same machine, the same 125B in its own NVFP4 build — the complete checkpoint from above, not a lighter quant flattering itself.

Same prompts, same salting, temperature 0, thinking off on both sides, three runs or more, counters read from the server. Before any of it meant anything I audited the context actually served. Ollama's prompt_eval_count exact-matched oMLX's prompt_tokens on all three 252k prompts: 255,716, 255,452, 255,474. Neither engine truncated.

  • short TTFT — 1.74 s vs 6.33–6.56 s
  • short prefill — 6,269 vs 1,688–1,751 tok/s; 3.6×
  • short decode — 144–147 vs 59.6–62.7 tok/s; 2.3×
  • 252k cold TTFT — 44.91 s vs 167–230 s; 3.7–5.1×
  • 252k cold prefill — 5,688 vs 1,111–1,568 tok/s
  • 252k cold decode — 82.1 vs 58.2 tok/s, and 24.3 under Ollama's own two-cache memory pressure
  • 252k warm decode — 90.7–91.1 vs 55.5 tok/s; +63%
  • needles in a 255,474-token prompt — 35.8 s and 15.8 s vs 162.3 s and 125.0 s. oMLX went in with a partial prefix cache (57,344 and 180,224 tokens), which is an advantage, and also the point: its cache is on disk and survives.

Quality next, because speed at depth is worth nothing if the model forgets the middle. Ten items, strict graders: arithmetic 391 / 3886 / 40119 exact on both engines; code 2/2; format 3/3; needles recovered at 25% and 75% depth inside a true 255,474-token context. 10/10 each. The NVFP4 arithmetic failures reported publicly did not reproduce here at temperature 0 with thinking off.

One row goes the other way, and it's real. Re-send the identical 252k prompt while the runner is still resident and Ollama returns a first token in 0.24 s — prompt evaluation 0.067 s, because the whole KV is already in RAM. oMLX's disk-paged restore of the same prompt is 1.14–2.83 s. For an exact repeat inside one process, Ollama is 5–10× faster to first token.

The bill is 149.2–155.3 GB peak wired and swap: 2.4 GB on the pressured 252k arm, 16.8 GB at its worst, 1.75 GB still resident afterwards. And the cache dies with the process. oMLX's survives a reboot.

Where the impression comes from. None of this is a story about a bad engine. It's a story about defaults, and about the way the engine gets measured.

One: the published benchmark is a different workload. Ollama's MLX announcement benchmarks a 35B-A3B on short prompts — a different model class in a different regime — and makes no long-context claim anywhere. The long-context claim was added later, by other people, by generalization.

Two: a context window nobody tells you about. The v0.40.16 notes say /v1/responses "no longer silently truncates by default", which is a sentence that tells you what it did before. The defaults are 4k below 24 GiB of VRAM and 32k up to 48 GiB. A 252k run that is secretly a 4k run looks spectacular. Credit where it's due: on this 256 GB box the default is an honest 262,144, and the audit above confirmed every token was counted. The failure mode lives on smaller machines, where nobody checks.

Three: speculation on by default. Ollama runs MTP adaptively, depth 0 to 7, with acceptance 0.32–0.42 measured at 127k. Compare that against an engine configured without speculation, or at the wrong depth, and you've manufactured a win without touching a kernel. The same default cuts both ways: there's a documented community report of Ollama's MTP-enabled 27B at 2× slower, the kind of number that never becomes folklore.

Four: the warm hit presented as general speed. 0.24 s is memorable. Nobody remembers the 149 GB.

Five: the origin story itself. The one direct Ultra-class head-to-head I could find — a thread in r/LocalLLM — began by claiming Ollama was faster and was corrected by its own author to oMLX about 1.7× faster. That's the mechanism in miniature: the first measurement travels; the correction doesn't.

Two checks against my own bias. MacStories published 74.7 tok/s after a 256K prompt, and this box measures 84–91 on the same class of workload, so the external baseline points the same way as the local one. And this machine's own Ollama logs from November 10 — predating all of this — show 46–48 tok/s decode at 127k and a 96 GiB peak. The fresh numbers agree with them. It was never a bad install.

Why fewer bytes can't explain a win. The 125B decays from 147 to 85 tok/s as context fills. That's +4.9 ms per token, 6.8 to 11.8. Decomposed: about 6% is extra KV and index bytes. About 94% is roughly 340 serialized kernel launches per cycle on the QSA indexer's scan → top-k → gather chain. Effective bandwidth is ~372 GB/s, about 31% of the 1.2 TB/s peak.

So at 252k this engine isn't bandwidth-bound; it's dispatch-bound. An engine that moves fewer bytes per token — NVFP4's whole argument — cannot get faster here. Only a different kernel schedule, or a different measurement, changes the number.

The 51B n-gram/PLE table that the llama.cpp-era writeups blamed costs about 2 KB per token here: a capacity cost, not a speed cost. And MTP off would be ~1.6× slower, so speculation isn't the problem either.

What made the numbers trustworthy. Six habits, none of them clever.

  1. Audit the tokens actually served — prompt_eval_count, prompt_tokens, cached_tokens. A benchmark without a truncation audit is a guess with decimals.
  2. Read server-side counters. Client SSE coalescing inflated decode by ~17% in our own rows.
  3. Warm up before recording. The first request after an install paid 10.49 s of Metal shader compilation; the same request then took 1.74 s.
  4. Separate cold from warm, and say which one you're quoting. They differ by ~19× on TTFT and by ~11% on decode.
  5. Match the sampling: temperature 0, thinking off on both sides, same prompts, same salt.
  6. Change one variable per run, and write down what you rejected. The rejections are the part that keeps you honest.

One more, for anyone reading memory: vm_stat's wired figure reads wrong while the server is idle. Use the server's own accounting.

The scoreboard, as plainly as I can put it.

  • speed — oMLX, on every row except the exact repeat
  • cold TTFT at 252k — oMLX, 3.7–5.1×
  • warm TTFT, exact repeat — Ollama, 0.24 s against 1.14–2.83 s
  • warm TTFT, after a restart — oMLX, because Ollama has nothing to restore
  • quality — tie, 10/10 each
  • memory — oMLX at 133.3 GB wired with swap flat; Ollama at 149.2–155.3 GB and into swap
  • default context on a 256 GB machine — tie, 262,144 both

Ollama's engine is good engineering. NVFP4 weights, a complete 180B checkpoint, the 26.82 GiB PLE table kept host-side so it stays out of the GPU set, adaptive MTP on by default, an honest default context on a machine that can hold it. Those are the choices of people who understand the hardware. My complaint is with the measurement culture around the engine, and with defaults that flatter it on machines that can't hold the context. Not with the engineers.

oMLX isn't finished either. Ollama's 0.24 s warm floor is better than our 1.14–2.83 s, and closing that gap is an open item here, not a solved one. The 147 → 85 decay is ~94% a serialized kernel-scan problem that nobody upstream has fused yet.

Part 2 covers two experiments that lost: the NVIDIA external draft and the oQ4h weights. They're more useful than the wins, because a rejected experiment with a receipt is worth more than an accepted one without.

The number you can defend is worth more than the number that travels.
